Universal Principles

These apply to every garment in this manual.

Order of operations

Almost every garment follows the same logic: work flat as long as possible, and join pieces from smallest to largest. The industry phrasing is "sew as small as you can, as flat as you can, for as long as you can." A typical sequence:

  1. Interfacing and stabilizing (fuse before anything else)
  2. Details that live on a single flat piece — darts, pleats, pockets, style seams, plackets
  3. Zippers and closures that go into a seam (easier flat)
  4. Joining seams — shoulders first, then the pieces that hang from them
  5. Collars, necklines, facings
  6. Sleeves
  7. Waist joins (bodice to skirt, waistbands)
  8. Hems
  9. Buttonholes, buttons, hooks — always last

Pressing is half of sewing

Press every seam twice: first flat as sewn (to meld the stitches), then open or to the side as the pattern directs. A seam that is sewn but not pressed looks homemade; the same seam pressed looks factory-made. Match iron temperature to fiber, and use a press cloth on anything delicate or prone to shine.

Stabilize before you sew

  • Staystitch curved edges (necklines, waistlines, armholes) within the seam allowance immediately after cutting, so they can't stretch out of shape.
  • Interface anything that needs body or takes stress: collars, cuffs, plackets, waistbands, facings, button areas.
  • Test every stitch, tension, and interfacing choice on a scrap of the actual fabric before touching the garment.

Machine vs. serger

  • Sewing machine builds the garment: construction seams, topstitching, zippers, buttonholes, anything needing precision.
  • Serger finishes raw edges on wovens (overlock each piece's edges before or after seaming) and can be the primary construction tool for knits (4-thread overlock seam).
  • The serger never replaces the machine where accuracy matters — sew first, then serge the allowance, for any seam you might need to alter or unpick.
