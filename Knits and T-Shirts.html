Knits and T-Shirts

T-shirts, sweatshirts, leggings, and activewear — where the serger becomes the primary construction machine. Knits don't fray, stretch as you sew, and need stitches that stretch with them. Part of the Sewing Manual.

fabric, right side wrong side clear elastic / stay tape serger / overlock machine stitching

Fabric

Easy

  • Cotton/spandex jersey (190 gsm+)
  • Interlock
  • French terry
  • Ponte

Harder

  • Lightweight rayon jersey
  • Slinky knits
  • Ribbing used as fabric

Activewear

  • Nylon/spandex or poly/spandex
  • 4-way stretch, good recovery
  • — see the activewear section below

Match the stretch

  • Check the pattern's stretch %
  • A 50%-stretch pattern is tight in 20%-stretch interlock

Machine Setup

  • Needles: ballpoint / jersey for normal knits, stretch needle for spandex-heavy fabric — these slide between fibers instead of piercing them (skipped stitches usually mean wrong needle).
  • Thread: polyester everywhere; cotton thread snaps when the seam stretches.
  • Machine stitches: narrow zigzag (0.5–1.0 wide, 2.5 long) or the lightning stitch for seams; never a plain straight stitch on anything that stretches.
  • Serger: 4-thread overlock is the construction seam. Set differential feed slightly above 1 if seams come out wavy.

Assembly Order — T-Shirt

1Stabilize the shoulders

Serge clear elastic or fusible stay tape into the shoulder seams.

These carry the shirt's weight and would stretch out otherwise.

2Shoulder seams

Serge both shoulder seams — the elastic from step 1 rides inside them.

The shirt is now a single flat piece, open at the neck, sides, and underarms.

3Neckband

Attach the band in the round now, while the rest of the shirt is flat and easy to handle (full technique below).

4Sleeves flat

Match the cap center to the shoulder seam and serge each sleeve in one pass — no easing, because the shirt is still open and flat.

5Side seams

One continuous pass on each side — from the sleeve hem, through the underarm, down to the bottom hem. Match the underarm seam so it lines up.

6Hems
  1. Press the hem allowance up; optionally fuse knit stay tape into the fold.
  2. Twin needle from the right side (or coverstitch).

Total: about six seams — the whole garment, which is why a t-shirt is the best first knit project.

Key Techniques

The neckband

The single technique that makes or breaks a t-shirt:

  • Cut the band 75–85% of the neckline length (ribbing: shorter end of range; jersey: longer), with the greatest stretch running lengthwise.
  • Sew the band into a loop, fold wrong sides together, press.
  • Quarter the band and the neckline with pins, match the quarter points (band seam at center back), and serge with the band on top, stretching only the band to fit between pins — never the shirt.
  • Press flat, then optionally topstitch the seam allowance down with a twin needle.
  • If it stands away from the body, the band was too long or you stretched the neckline; if it flips out, too short. Cut a test band — it costs a scrap.

Hemming knits

Twin needle (stretch twin, 4.0/75) gives the ready-to-wear double line: two straight rows on top, zigzag bobbin underneath that stretches. Loosen needle tension slightly and use a slightly longer stitch to avoid the "tunnel" ridge between the rows; fusible knit stay tape in the fold eliminates it entirely.

No serger needed (backup)

Everything here also works on a plain machine: a narrow zigzag for seams and a zigzag or twin needle for hems. The serger is faster and cleaner, not mandatory.

Serger Notes

  • The 4-thread overlock is the construction seam for every join above — it sews, trims, and finishes in one pass.
  • If seams come out wavy, raise the differential feed slightly above 1 so the feed dogs ease the fabric instead of stretching it.
  • Use a ballpoint serger needle and polyester thread; cotton serger thread snaps under stretch.

Advanced: Activewear and Leggings

  • Seams under tension: 4-thread serge, or lightning stitch; for leggings, sew the crotch/gusset seam twice.
  • Clear elastic is the workhorse — serge it into waistband edges, leg openings, and any seam that needs recovery without bulk. Stretch it slightly taut as you sew.
  • Waistbands: wide elastic inside a fabric casing, or a tall self-fabric band with clear elastic in the top seam. The Last Stitch's Aila sewalong covers both.
  • Gusset: the diamond at the leggings crotch; sew it to both legs flat, then close everything in one pass.
  • Negative ease is the fit model: leggings are drafted smaller than the body; don't size up because the flat pieces look tiny.
  • Hem with a twin needle or a wide zigzag — and hem before the final inseam pass when the pattern allows; it's much easier flat.
